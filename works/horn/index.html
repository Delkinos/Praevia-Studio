Before the guide

Three things worth knowing first

A quick primer so the plates ahead make more sense.

01

It's keratin, not bone

True horns are a bony core sheathed in compressed keratin — the same material as fingernails. Unlike antlers, that sheath never falls off and keeps growing for life.

02

Shape follows fighting style

Spiraled horns lock together in twisting duels, curved horns clash head-on, and straight rapier horns are built for fencing at a distance. Form is a record of combat.

03

Growth rings tell age

Many horned species lay down a new ridge each year, the way a tree lays down rings — count the ridges on a bighorn and you're reading its birth year.

Frequently Confused

Horn, or antler?

They look related. Biologically, they aren't.

Horn

  • Permanent — grows continuously and is never shed.
  • A bony core wrapped in a keratin sheath.
  • Usually unbranched, though it may curve or spiral.
  • Found on both males and females in most species (cattle, sheep, goats, antelope).
  • Growth slows but never fully stops once maturity is reached.

Owners in this guide

Bighorn Sheep, Gemsbok, Greater Kudu, Alpine Ibex, Addax, Markhor, Water Buffalo, and Pronghorn are all true horn-bearers — along with rhinos, whose horn is unique in having no bony core at all, just solid compressed keratin.

Antler

  • Temporary — grown fresh each year and shed after mating season.
  • Pure bone, covered in blood-rich "velvet" while growing.
  • Usually branched into multiple points or tines.
  • Found almost exclusively on males (reindeer are the exception).
  • Regrows larger each year until the animal passes its prime.

None of these, here

Deer, elk, moose, and caribou grow antlers rather than horns — which is exactly why you won't find them in this guide. This collection is horn-bearers only.
